Do clusters of insider purchases predict stock returns?

When two or more of a company's insiders buy its shares on the open market within two weeks, does the stock beat the market afterwards, once trading costs are paid? EDGAR-SENTINEL reads every SEC Form 4 filing since 2006 to find these clusters, measures what happened next and scores the newest ones.

Pilot sample (2006–2011), survivors only — prototype, not research results. Returns are measured only for 2006–2011 events, at companies that still trade today. The 2012–2026 study stays sealed until its plan is pre-registered.

Pilot answer

Yes, modestly. Stocks bought by insider clusters beat small caps by +3.5% over the next three months, after costs.

The typical (median) event gained +0.6%, and 2009 carries much of the average (+18.0% that year). Solo CEO/CFO purchases did about as well (+4.1%). These numbers cover survivors only; the pre-registered 2012–2026 study will test the idea properly.

What happened after insiders bought

Each event is bought at the opening price on the first trading day after the Form 4 was filed. The abnormal return is the stock's return minus the benchmark's over the same days. Costs are a round-trip bid–ask spread estimated from each stock's daily high and low prices.

Average abnormal return around the purchase

Trading days relative to entry. Left of the dashed line, the line shows how far the price was above the entry price: insiders tend to buy after a fall. The shaded area around each line is its 95% range (blue for clusters, orange for solo CEO/CFO): the true average very likely lies inside it. Where a range stays above 0%, the gain is unlikely to be luck. Ranges widen further out because longer-run returns vary more, and the solo range is wider because it has fewer events. Ranges come from resampling whole calendar months, so events in the same month move together. Click a name in the legend to hide that line and its range.

Machine learning · trained 2006–2010, tested on 2011

Can a model tell the good clusters from the bad?

Each model predicts whether an event's 63-day abnormal return against small caps, after costs, will be positive. It sees only what was known on the filing date: who bought, how much, how their holdings changed, recent insider selling, and the stock's recent trading.

Pilot sample (2006–2011), survivors only — prototype, not research results. The model is trained on a small, survivors-only sample. Treat its scores as a demonstration of the method.

Pilot verdict

Not yet. In this small pilot the insider details add little beyond market data.

On 2011 events the selected model ranked winners only slightly better than chance (AUC 0.54); a model that sees only market data scored 0.56. Its probabilities were no sharper than always predicting the base rate (log loss 0.698 against 0.693). The top fifth of scored events earned +3.3% against +1.4% for all 2011 events, but with about 84 events in that group the gap is fragile. The full study has about five times as many development events, plus delisted stocks.

How the model was built

Outcome
1 if the 63-day abnormal return against IWM, after the estimated round-trip cost, is above zero; otherwise 0. Base rate 52%.
Training
1,621 events from 2006–2010 whose 63-day window ended before 2011 (purged, so no training outcome overlaps the test year).
Test
421 events from 2011, scored once.
Candidates
Regularised logistic regression (median imputation, missing-value flags, standardised inputs) and LightGBM (small fixed settings, no tuning).
Baseline
The selected family trained on market data only: liquidity, recent returns, momentum, distance from the 52-week high, volatility and spread.
Live scores
The selected model, refitted on all 2,083 pilot events, scores the Jul–Sep 2026 events using only data up to each filing date. No outcome is computed for any event after 2011.

Score & features · what goes into each score

How the score is made

The definition of the score, every input the models consider, and what each model produced when tested.

Pilot sample (2006–2011), survivors only — prototype, not research results. The models were trained on a small, survivors-only sample. Their weights describe 2006–2011, not a proven rule.

What the score means

Score = the model's estimated probability that
stock return − IWM return − trading cost > 0
over the 63 trading days after the Form 4 filing

Buy and hold
Bought at the opening price on the first trading day after the filing date, the first price any investor could act on, and held for 63 trading days (about three months).
Benchmark
IWM, the iShares Russell 2000 ETF, which tracks small US companies. Most stocks insiders cluster-buy are small, so this is the fair comparison.
Trading cost
A round-trip bid–ask spread estimated from the stock's daily high, low and closing prices in the 20 days before the filing (median 0.60%).
Reading it
50% is a coin flip. In the pilot, 52% of events beat IWM after costs, so a score near that is "average". Pilot scores ranged from 43% to 64% for the middle 90% of events (median 52%).
Percentile
"Higher than 53% of pilot events" means the score beats the scores of 53% of the 2,083 pilot events.
Who makes it
The selected logistic regression, refitted on all 2,083 pilot events (2006–2011), using the 19 features below, each measured with data available on the filing date only.
Partial data
Four or more of the 19 inputs were missing, for example a stock listed less than a year ago has no momentum. Missing inputs are filled with the pilot median and flagged.
No score
The company no longer trades (no prices); the reported purchase price was outside that day's trading range (price check); or the company is a fund or blank-check company (excluded).
How good it is
Only slightly better than chance so far: AUC 0.54 on the 2011 test. See the comparison below and the Model tab.

    Method & limits

    How the prototype works and what it cannot tell you yet

    The question

    Corporate insiders must report their trades to the SEC on Form 4 within two business days. The project asks whether purchases made by several insiders at once, or by the CEO or CFO alone, predict that the stock will beat the market, after the costs a small investor would pay.

    How an event is found

    • Clean purchases: original Form 4s only; open-market purchases (transaction code P) of common stock; no ticked 10b5-1 plan box and no footnote pointing to a pre-planned plan, dividend reinvestment or a company share offering; at least $10,000 per filing; filed within 30 days of the trade; trade price at least $2.
    • Cluster: clean purchases from at least two buyers filed within 14 calendar days, totalling at least $100,000.
    • Solo CEO/CFO: a CEO or CFO buying at least $100,000 with no other insider buying in the window.
    • Cooldown: no second event of the same type at the same company within 91 days.

    How returns are measured

    • Entry at the opening price on the first trading day after the filing date, the first price any investor could act on.
    • Abnormal return: the stock's return minus that of IWM (small caps) or SPY (S&P 500) over the same days.
    • Tradability: the as-traded price must be at least $2 and the average daily dollar volume at least $100,000 on the filing date.
    • Cost: a round-trip bid–ask spread estimated from daily high and low prices (Abdi & Ranaldo, 2017).
    • Uncertainty: 95% bands from resampling blocks of whole calendar months, because events in the same month share market moves.

    Why only 2006–2011

    The full study measures 2012–2022 for development and keeps 2023–2026 as a sealed test. Its rules are locked and time-stamped before any of those returns are computed. This prototype therefore uses only the years before the study window, so showing these results cannot influence the real test.

    What is missing here

    • Delisted companies. 6,937 pilot events shrink to 2,083 because only companies still trading today have free price history. Failures and takeovers are both missing, so the direction of the bias is unknown, though failures usually matter more.
    • Simplified insider rules. Co-filing funds and their managers can count as separate buyers here; the full study merges them using only filings made up to each date.
    • Simple costs and benchmarks. No price-impact cost, no industry-adjusted returns and no risk-factor checks yet.
    • A small, untuned model. About 2,083 events and fixed settings.
